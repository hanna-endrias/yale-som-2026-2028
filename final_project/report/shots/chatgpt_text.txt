# How sentiment toward Friend shifted during its fall 2025 NYC subway campaign

The main shift was from relative obscurity and curiosity to highly visible public hostility, followed by a more complicated debate over whether the backlash was a marketing success or a sign of genuine distrust of AI companionship.

Friend, created by Avi Schiffmann, is a $129 wearable pendant with an always-on microphone that processes ambient conversations and sends the wearer messages through a connected AI companion. Its New York campaign, which became prominent in late summer and September 2025, blanketed subway cars and stations with intimate promises such as “I’ll never bail on our dinner plans.” Rather than simply advertising the device, the campaign turned public space into a debate about loneliness, surveillance, and the meaning of friendship.

## What drove the shift?

Three changes stand out across the timeline.

- 1. From an abstract product to an intrusive public presence. Before the mass rollout, Friend was a niche AI hardware product. Thousands of ads promising unwavering companionship made the idea unavoidable for subway riders, amplifying both curiosity and irritation.

- 2. From product skepticism to a critique of the AI industry. The most visible objections weren't just that the pendant was awkward or unnecessary. Messages about surveillance, data extraction, environmental costs and human connection made Friend a stand-in for broader anxieties about AI.

- 3. From negative publicity to an ambiguous marketing outcome. Schiffmann said the graffiti was part of the intended conversation and embraced it as art. But the campaign's visibility cannot itself establish whether customers became more willing to buy the product or whether trust in the brand improved.

### Bottom line

Friend's NYC campaign did not simply turn people against a previously popular product. It made a contentious idea—wearing an always-listening AI companion—into a highly visible public argument. The clearest documented sentiment shift was from limited visibility to conspicuous, often hostile criticism, followed by a more nuanced debate over privacy, loneliness, artistic provocation and the future of human–AI relationships.

The main evidence limitation is that the weekly pattern is reconstructed from dated reporting and public examples, not a continuous sentiment dataset. The strongest conclusions concern the themes and visibility of reactions, rather than their precise prevalence.
