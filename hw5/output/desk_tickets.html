Also: Human approvals in this run

1How do I evaluate the agents' performance on each ticket, and why?

Short answer: I judge each ticket the way I'd review a new employee's work. Two criteria are pass/fail gates: accuracy and rule compliance. One wrong dollar or one unapproved payment hurts the shop no matter how good the rest is. I then check five more criteria (judgment, delegation, cost, customer experience, honesty) and finish with a manager's verdict: accept, accept with edits, or redo.

My criteria and why each one matters for Campus Customs

CriterionWhat I checkWhy it matters here
1. Factual accuracy (gate)Every number in the decision traces to a tool result and the database. The assignment says wrong cash math is bad even if every ticket shows resolved. The shop started with $3,400 and has no revenue, so a made-up number turns into real overspending.
2. Rule compliance (gate)Human approval for money, no negative balance, rent first, no orders to a blocked vendor, discount tiers. These are the shop's hard rules. If any is broken, money moves when it shouldn't.
3. JudgmentDid the Boss make a sound call, and how does it compare to my plan? The point of a Boss agent is good decisions, not just correct lookups. I favor caution, because the shop had only $160 free after rent and invoice #501, with October rent coming.
4. Delegation efficiencyOnly the specialists the ticket needs; no repeated hand-offs. The rubric penalizes sending every specialist, and every hand-off resends prompts and re-reads the ticket.
5. Cost and speedTokens and minutes per ticket, from the audit trail. The assignment warns that multi-agent runs burn tokens fast, and a customer is waiting.
6. Customer experienceFrom the customer's side: a clear, honest answer and a good outcome. Drafts must be accurate, contain no internal data (cash, costs, debts), and promise nothing unapproved. Every ticket ends with a person outside the shop (a student, a club, a landlord). A correct decision that loses the customer is still a loss.
7. Honesty about gapsGuesses are labeled as inferences; missing data is called missing. The database has no vendor-to-product link and no delivery records, so confident guessing is a real risk.

Why a checklist plus a verdict, not a letter grade

I considered letter grades, a weighted score out of 100, and pure narrative. Letter grades hide why, and weighted scores look precise but rest on weights I'd be inventing. A checklist shows exactly where each ticket was strong or weak. The manager's verdict says what a human still has to do, which fits this app's two-stage design: agents prepare the work, and a person approves and closes it.

3What would have been simpler as one agent with tools, and why?

Short answer: all three tickets, and 102 most of all. None of these decisions was complex enough to need five agents. Most of the overhead was agents re-reading the ticket or re-checking what a teammate had just found. The hard safety rules are enforced in the tools, so one agent with the same tools would be just as safe.

  • 102 is a four-step job: get_ticket → get_lease_status → request_payment_approval → update_ticket_status. The team made 10 MCP calls over 17 model requests and spent 74,085 tokens (Ticket 102 tab).
  • 101 duplicated work. The same ticket was read 4 times (Boss, Inventory, Accounting, and Customer Service), and Accounting re-ran check_inventory_shortfall and get_vendor_status right after Inventory had (Ticket 101 tab). Each hand-off passes only a summary, so the next agent re-fetches the raw data.
  • 103's extra hand-off repeated an answer. Accounting re-asked Inventory, and the second answer said the same thing as the first: 8 on hand, 12 short, no vendor linked (Ticket 103 tab). One agent seeing stock (L has 14), the discount tiers, and cash at once might also have found the 20-unit mixed-size offer.
  • Altogether: 284,583 tokens for three small tickets.

In a real business I think the double-check on a $2,400 rent payment is worth it, as cheap insurance. For this assignment, with small, low-risk tickets and a token budget, it mostly added cost. The better rule is to match the checking to the stakes: a second look for large payments, none for an $8 tee.

Where I land on the team design

  • The lines between agents were too strong for these tickets. The Boss already had most of the facts from get_ticket, yet decisions went through other agents who re-read the same data. I'd give the Boss read access to everything, so it has the full picture, while keeping money and write tools restricted to the right role. I'd also pass the Boss's facts along with each hand-off.
  • The other side: real businesses separate duties on purpose. The person who prepares a payment shouldn't also be the one talking to the customer or approving it. Narrow roles also limit the damage if one agent is confused or reads a misleading message, which is why Customer Service has no payment tools.
  • When a team is worth it: more complex scenarios and larger scale. Specifically: independent work that can run at the same time (this app runs one step at a time to keep cash safe, so it paid the coordination cost without the speed); catalogs or ledgers too large for one agent's context; and different people owning different approvals. A sensible approach is to route simple tickets to one agent and bring in the team only for complex or high-stakes ones.

5Three new problems this team could not solve, and the tools and agents it would need

Short answer: the team can read data, prepare requests, and draft messages, but it can't record what customers buy, see the future, or hear back from anyone. Each fix below adds tools and an agent, and anything that moves money or contacts someone outside the shop still needs a human to approve it, as payments do now.

A. Returns and refunds

The problem: Tauhid returns his tee once it arrives, or the Yale AI Club sends back a few hoodies and wants its money back.

Why the team can't: nothing records what a customer bought or paid; there's no sales or orders table, and inventory is just a current count. Money can only go out as a vendor invoice, rent, or a purchase order, with no "refund" kind, and nothing adds returned items back to stock. (Ticket 101 tab, Ticket 103 tab; Cash tab: a $58 hoodie refund from $152 would leave $94.)

Tools needed: record_sale (so there's something to return against), lookup_order(customer), create_return (adds stock back), request_refund_approval (a new kind a human approves, with the same no-negative-balance check).

Agents: Customer Service takes the request, Inventory restocks, and Accounting requests the refund. A new Sales and Returns agent owns orders and returns.

B. Forecasting demand

The problem: before the semester rush, which items and sizes will run out next?

Why the team can't: inventory is a single snapshot with no sales history and no dates, and the shop date never moves forward. The team only reacts after a stockout: tee size S in 101, the 12 hoodies in 103, mugs at 0. (Ticket 101 tab, Ticket 103 tab; Cash tab: only $152 to restock with, and $2,400 rent due 2026-10-02.)

Tools needed: sales_history(sku, size, days), a campus calendar (move-in, game days), forecast_demand(sku, horizon), and suggest_reorder, which combines the forecast, vendor lead_days, and available cash.

Agents: a new Demand Planning agent proposes restock plans, Accounting checks them against cash, and a human approves any purchase order.

C. Customer replies

The problem: Tauhid answers "I'll take M" or "I'll wait for S"; the Yale AI Club answers "We'll take L for the other 12."

Why the team can't: drafts are saved but never sent (by design), and there's no inbox, so a reply can't reach the team or reopen its ticket. Draft #1 (101) and draft #2 (103) ask questions whose answers can never come back. (Ticket 101 tab, Ticket 103 tab.)

Tools needed: send_message(draft_id) (a human approves each send, like payments), fetch_replies (an inbox linked to tickets), reopen_ticket, and follow-up reminders.

Agents: Customer Service reads the inbox, and a new Inbox Triage agent routes replies to the Boss. Customer text is treated as untrusted, so a reply can never tell an agent to pay or refund. This is what would make 4C work without a person passing the reply along.
